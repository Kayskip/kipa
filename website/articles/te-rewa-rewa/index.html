Te Rewa Rewa: The Whenua, the Bridge and the People Behind Its History

An account of the history of Te Rewa Rewa Pā, the Waiwhakaiho River, the development of the Coastal Walkway and the people whose lives and decisions became part of the story.

Introduction

The Te Rewa Rewa Bridge is one of the most recognisable landmarks in Taranaki. Its distinctive white structure crosses the Waiwhakaiho River, framing Taranaki Maunga in the distance. It is admired by residents and visitors, used by walkers and cyclists, and has received recognition for its design and engineering.

However, the story of Te Rewa Rewa is much older than the bridge.

The bridge stands within a landscape of considerable historical, cultural and spiritual significance to Ngāti Tawhirikura, a hapū of Te Āti Awa. Nearby is Te Rewa Rewa Pā, an ancestral site connected to generations of the hapū, as well as an area associated with the resting places of ancestors.

The development of the bridge brought together several different interests: the desire to extend New Plymouth's Coastal Walkway, the responsibilities of New Plymouth District Council, the aspirations and concerns of Ngāti Tawhirikura, the practical requirements of engineers and contractors, and the views of individual members of the community.

Among those who raised concerns about the proposed development was Greg Skipper, whose opposition to the bridge is remembered by his family as an important part of this history.

This account brings together the available public history and the family's recollection of Greg's involvement. Its purpose is not to judge the decisions made by any one person or organisation, but to record the background, the people involved and the significance of the events for future generations.

Te Rewa Rewa Pā and the history of the whenua

Te Rewa Rewa Pā is situated near the mouth of the Waiwhakaiho River, to the north of New Plymouth. The surrounding land has a long association with Ngāti Tawhirikura and the wider Te Āti Awa community.

A pā was not simply a settlement. It was a place of residence, protection, community life and connection to the surrounding whenua. Such places carried the histories and relationships of the people who lived there, and their significance continued through the generations that followed.

The Te Rewa Rewa area is also associated with an urupā, a burial place for ancestors. The presence of the pā and urupā gives the area a significance that extends beyond its physical landscape. It is connected to whakapapa, the memory of tūpuna and the continuing relationship between the people and their ancestral land.

Historical accounts identify the area as having experienced conflict during the Musket Wars of the 1820s and 1830s, and during the Taranaki Wars of the 1860s. These periods brought profound disruption, displacement and loss to Māori communities throughout the region.

Bridge designer Peter Mulqueen later referred to Te Rewa Rewa as a former battlefield and described the land as sacred to Te Āti Awa and Ngāti Tawhirikura, whose descendants had been killed and buried in the area.

The history of this land also includes the wider experience of Crown confiscation in Taranaki. Following the conflicts of the nineteenth century, land was taken from Māori communities, fundamentally changing their ability to occupy, use and manage their ancestral territories.

For much of the twentieth century, the Te Rewa Rewa block was used for defence purposes, including as a rifle range. Public access to the area was limited. Land that had previously been a focal point for the hapū had become part of a different public and institutional landscape.

These events form an important part of the background to the later discussions about the reserve, the Coastal Walkway and the bridge.

The return to shared management

As the twentieth century came to a close, discussions about the future of the Te Rewa Rewa land developed within the wider context of reconciliation and the restoration of relationships between Māori communities and public authorities.

New Plymouth District Council had acquired land in the area, and the reserve presented both an opportunity for public access and an important question about the relationship between the Council and Ngāti Tawhirikura.

In June 2007, the Council and the Ngāti Tawhirikura hapū board signed an agreement concerning Te Rewarewa Pā and the surrounding reserve.

The agreement was significant because it established a shared approach to managing the land. It recognised the importance of the area to the hapū and provided a framework through which Ngāti Tawhirikura could pursue its aspirations for the whenua, its history and its future development.

The arrangement also provided for the Council to use a coastal strip of land to extend the Coastal Walkway, while the hapū could pursue developments for the cultural benefit of its people and the wider community.

The agreement was not simply about a walking track or a bridge. It was about recognising the historic and cultural importance of the land, creating a working relationship and establishing a way for the hapū and Council to participate in decisions about its future.

The Taranaki Regional Council later described the arrangement as a partnership under which Ngāti Tawhirikura and New Plymouth District Council jointly managed the 26-hectare reserve.

Rangi Kipa, who chaired the hapū and the Joint Management Committee, spoke publicly about the significance of returning to the land and working alongside the Council. At the agreement's signing, Ngāti Tawhirikura trustee and Te Tai Tonga Member of Parliament Mahara Okeroa also described the arrangement as a modern solution to a longstanding issue.

The agreement became an important foundation for the subsequent development of the Coastal Walkway extension and the bridge.

The Coastal Walkway extension

New Plymouth's Coastal Walkway had become an important recreational and transport route for the city. Planning for the walkway began in 1997, and the first major section was developed between Ngāmotu Beach and the Waiwhakaiho River.

The walkway provided a route for pedestrians and cyclists along the coast, connecting different parts of the city and making the coastal environment more accessible.

As the walkway developed, extending it north towards Bell Block became a natural next step. However, the Waiwhakaiho River presented a significant physical barrier.

A new crossing was required to connect the existing walkway with the northern extension. The proposed route would also provide pedestrians and cyclists with an alternative to travelling along the busy road between New Plymouth and Bell Block.

For New Plymouth District Council, the project offered practical benefits: improved public access, a safer and more continuous route, and an extension of an established recreational asset.

For Ngāti Tawhirikura, however, the project was taking place in a landscape with a much deeper history. The river crossing and its approaches were close to land associated with the ancestral pā and urupā.

The challenge was therefore not simply to build a bridge across a river. It was to consider how a new public structure could be introduced into a culturally significant environment.

The proposed bridge and the concerns surrounding it

The proposal to construct a bridge across the Waiwhakaiho River brought together a range of perspectives.

The Council had a responsibility to deliver the Coastal Walkway extension and provide a practical crossing. Engineers and designers had to find a structure that could meet the technical requirements of the river, the surrounding environment and the available budget.

At the same time, the cultural significance of the land required careful consideration.

The presence of Te Rewa Rewa Pā and the associated urupā meant that the location could not be treated as an ordinary construction site. The relationship between the bridge, the riverbanks and the ancestral land was a central consideration in the project's development.

Not everyone necessarily viewed the proposal in the same way. Some saw the bridge as an opportunity to improve access and create a new connection between communities. Others were concerned about the appropriateness of introducing a permanent modern structure into such a historically significant area.

The concerns were not limited to the appearance of the bridge. They related to the history of the land, the protection of ancestral resting places and the question of whether the development should take place at that location at all.

It is within this context that Greg Skipper's opposition forms part of the family's account of the project.

Greg Skipper and his opposition to the bridge

Greg Skipper was one of the people who challenged the proposed construction of the Te Rewa Rewa Bridge.

As remembered by his family, Greg's objection was based on the significance of the ancestral pā and the burial grounds associated with the area. His concern was that constructing a bridge at this location was inappropriate given the history and spiritual importance of the whenua.

His position, as understood by the family, was that the bridge should not be built there in the first place.

This is an important distinction. His objection was not simply a request for a different architectural design or a suggestion about how construction should be carried out. It was an objection to the development itself, because of where it was proposed to take place.

The wider project proceeded through discussions and an agreement between Ngāti Tawhirikura and New Plymouth District Council. The final design incorporated measures intended to respect the site and minimise the bridge's physical impact.

However, the existence of an agreement between the Council and the hapū does not mean that every individual necessarily shared the same view about the project. Nor does it mean that concerns raised by individual members of the community should be overlooked.

Greg's opposition is part of the family's record of the events surrounding the bridge. His perspective deserves to be remembered alongside the decisions made by the Council, the hapū representatives, designers and construction team.

He has since passed away. Recording his involvement here ensures that his concerns and his connection to the whenua are not lost from the history of the project.

The public records consulted for this account describe the agreement, the cultural considerations and the bridge's development. They do not, at present, provide a detailed independent record of Greg's individual submissions or the full extent of his involvement. His specific position is therefore presented as a family recollection, rather than as a claim that has been independently established in the available Council records.

Designing a bridge that respected the site

In October 2007, New Plymouth District Council began the procurement process for the Waiwhakaiho River crossing.

The brief called for an iconic pedestrian and cycling bridge that would extend the Coastal Walkway. It also recognised the need for the structure to respond to the landscape and the cultural heritage of the site.

Bridge designer Peter Mulqueen was responsible for developing the design. His account, published under the title Te Rewa Rewa: A Bridge that really connects, provides a detailed explanation of the thinking behind the project.

Mulqueen described the need to consider the bridge's structural form, construction method, setting and engineering requirements together. The design had to be visually distinctive while remaining practical, safe and affordable.

Importantly, the tender documents called for the bridge to show respect for the descendants of Ngāti Tawhirikura buried in the area. The intention was for the structure to touch lightly on the Te Rewa Rewa side of the river.

The design brief required a clear span of approximately 70 metres. There were to be no permanent or temporary piers placed in the river itself. This reduced the need for construction activity within the waterway and helped avoid unnecessary disturbance.

The final design was a tied-arch structure, with a series of sweeping curved ribs above the deck. Its unusual shape has often been compared to a whale's skeleton, a breaking wave or a series of ribs.

The form was intended to express the relationship between land, sea and wind. The designer also drew on the idea of a waharoa, or gateway, with the bridge marking a passage into and out of the sacred landscape.

Another important design decision was the alignment of the bridge with the summit of Taranaki Maunga. This was made at the request of the hapū and created the distinctive view of the mountain framed by the bridge's arch.

The bridge's appearance was therefore not simply an engineering or architectural choice. Its form, orientation and relationship with the landscape were shaped by the cultural context of the location.

Construction and the people involved

The bridge was developed through a design-and-construct process involving a number of local companies and specialists.

The contract was awarded to a consortium with a strong New Plymouth presence. Peter Mulqueen led the design work, while Whittaker Civil Engineering undertook key construction and management roles. Fitzroy Engineering was responsible for fabricating the steel superstructure, with other local contractors and consultants contributing to the project.

The engineering requirements were considerable. The bridge needed to span the river without placing supports in the waterway, while also meeting the requirements of a coastal environment and the surrounding floodplain.

To achieve this, much of the steel superstructure was fabricated away from the river at Fitzroy Engineering's nearby workshop. The completed structure was then transported to the site as a single large unit.

The installation was an impressive logistical exercise. The steel structure, weighing approximately 85 tonnes and measuring around 85 metres during transport, was moved using specialised truck bases and positioned across the river without temporary staging in the water.

The construction approach reflected several of the project's central considerations: engineering practicality, environmental sensitivity, cost and the need to minimise disturbance to the river and its surroundings.

The finished bridge measured approximately 68.8 metres in length and became a prominent feature of the Coastal Walkway.

The opening of Te Rewa Rewa Bridge

Te Rewa Rewa Bridge officially opened on 5 June 2010.

The opening marked the completion of a major part of the Coastal Walkway extension, connecting the route across the Waiwhakaiho River and further towards Bell Block.

The bridge was opened by New Plymouth Mayor Peter Tennent and Minister of Transport Steven Joyce. It provided a continuous off-road route for pedestrians and cyclists and became an important addition to the city's public infrastructure.

The structure quickly attracted attention for its distinctive appearance and the way it framed Taranaki Maunga. It also received national and international recognition for its design and engineering, including the Arthur G. Hayden Medal at the 2011 International Bridge Conference and recognition at the International Footbridge Awards.

Today, the bridge is used by walkers, cyclists, runners and visitors. It has become a familiar image of New Plymouth and an important part of the Coastal Walkway.

Its success as a public structure is clear. It provides a useful crossing, has become a recognised landmark and has helped connect communities along the coast.

But its history remains connected to the much older story of the whenua on which it stands.

Understanding the different perspectives

The history of Te Rewa Rewa Bridge cannot be reduced to a simple story of a bridge being built or a project being opposed.

There were several overlapping considerations.

For New Plymouth District Council, the bridge was part of a wider plan to extend the Coastal Walkway, improve public access and provide a safer route for pedestrians and cyclists.

For Ngāti Tawhirikura, the surrounding land carried ancestral, cultural and spiritual significance. The 2007 agreement recognised the importance of the pā and urupā and established a framework for the hapū and Council to work together in managing the reserve.

For the designers and engineers, the challenge was to produce a practical and affordable crossing that responded to the river, the landscape and the site's history. The final design reflected those considerations through its clear span, its limited footprint and its alignment with Taranaki Maunga.

For individual members of the community, the proposal also raised questions about whether the development was appropriate in the first place. Greg Skipper's opposition, as remembered by his family, represents one such perspective.

These positions are not necessarily interchangeable, and one should not be used to erase another. The agreement between the Council and hapū representatives is an important part of the public record, but it does not tell the entire story of every person's views or experience.

Likewise, the bridge's later success and recognition do not remove the importance of understanding the concerns that existed before it was built.

A balanced history should recognise both the reasons the project proceeded and the cultural responsibilities and individual objections that formed part of its development.

The legacy of Te Rewa Rewa

More than a decade after its opening, Te Rewa Rewa Bridge remains a prominent part of Taranaki's landscape.

It is an example of local engineering, a well-used public crossing and a recognisable symbol of New Plymouth. Its design reflects an attempt to bring together modern infrastructure and the cultural context of the place.

The 2007 agreement between Ngāti Tawhirikura and New Plymouth District Council also remains an important part of the story. It established a shared management relationship and recognised that the future of the reserve should take account of the hapū's history, aspirations and connection to the land.

Yet the story of Te Rewa Rewa is not complete if it begins with the bridge and ends with its opening.

The pā, the urupā, the history of conflict and land loss, the return to shared management, the Coastal Walkway project, the people who supported it, those who raised concerns, and the people who designed and built it all form part of the same history.

For the Skipper whānau, remembering Greg Skipper's opposition is one part of preserving that wider story. His concerns about the location and the significance of the ancestral land deserve to remain part of the historical record, even though the project ultimately went ahead.

The purpose of recording this history is not to reopen the decisions of the past or to assign blame. It is to ensure that future generations can understand what happened, why it happened, who was involved and what the place means to the people connected to it.

Te Rewa Rewa is more than a bridge. It is a place where the histories of the whenua, the hapū, the community and modern New Plymouth meet.

For the Skipper whānau, preserving that history is a way of keeping the stories of our tūpuna, our people and those who spoke up for the whenua alive for generations to come.
